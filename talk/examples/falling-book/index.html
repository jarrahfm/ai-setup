Why do things fall together?

Drop a hammer and a feather. On Earth, the hammer lands first. On the Moon, they land together. This page shows you why, and every picture on it is one you can run.

1A hammer and a feather on the Moon

In 1971, astronaut David Scott was on the Moon with the Apollo 15 mission.1 2 He held out a hammer and a feather, about 1.6 m above the ground, and let go of both at the same time.1 The hammer was 1.32 kg. The feather was 0.03 kg.1 They hit the ground together.1

On Earth, the feather lands much later. The air slows it down.

Air resistance is the push of the air against anything that moves through it, working against the motion.2

On the Moon there is almost no air. NASA says the hammer and the feather were essentially in a vacuum.1

A vacuum is a space with no matter in it, not even air.6

With no air resistance, nothing held the feather back, so it fell at the same rate as the hammer.1 Galileo had reached the same idea hundreds of years earlier: things dropped together fall at the same rate, however heavy they are.1

2Gravity makes falling things speed up

Gravity is the force that pulls objects toward the centre of a planet or a moon.5

When you let go of something, gravity makes it fall faster and faster.

Acceleration is how quickly the speed of something changes.3

When air resistance is small enough to ignore, all objects in the same place fall with the same acceleration, however heavy they are.2 That is why the hammer and the feather stayed side by side on the Moon.

Read the graph. Each line starts flat, then gets steeper and steeper. A steeper line means the ball is falling faster: that is acceleration.

The Earth line gets steep much sooner, because gravity on Earth is stronger. Its acceleration is about 9.81 m/s² on Earth and about 1.62 m/s² on the Moon.4 The unit m/s² means: every second, the speed grows by this many metres per second.

3Stronger gravity, shorter fall

For something that starts still and falls with no air resistance, this rule gives the time of the fall:2

t = √2h ÷ g

t is the time in seconds, h is the height in metres, and g is the acceleration due to gravity in m/s².

Try Earth × 4. Gravity four times stronger makes the fall take half as long, not a quarter. The time follows the square root of g.

What the pictures leave out

  • The feather's path through air in Figure 1 is drawn to show the idea. It is not measured.
  • The hammer in Figure 1, and every ball in Figures 2 and 3, falls as if there were no air at all. Real air pushes back on every falling object, the hammer too.2
  • Sources do not always agree exactly. NASA gives 1.624 m/s² for gravity on the Moon4; the OpenStax textbook uses 1.67 m/s².2 This page uses NASA's numbers. With 1.67, the Moon fall from 1.6 m takes 1.38 s instead of 1.40 s.
  • Sideways sizes and distances in the pictures are not to scale. Heights are.
